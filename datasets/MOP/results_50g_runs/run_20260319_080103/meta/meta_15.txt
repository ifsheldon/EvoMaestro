# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The new strongest result is still the same core family: direct Gaussian deformation of a straight-line baseline with uniform x-sampling, but with an even stronger school/base tradeoff.**  
  The current best, **Gaussian School Avoidance with Base Attraction**, achieves **22.67** with **F1=1.36, F2=0.49, F3=33.99**, clearly improving on the previously best-reported **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path** at **29.85**. Its implementation is very simple: compute `y_line`, apply a **fixed signed Gaussian repulsion** from the school (`push_dir * 35.0 * w_s`), then apply a **Gaussian-weighted pull toward the base** using the already-shifted `y`. This confirms and strengthens the earlier insight that **plain, evaluator-aligned Gaussian shaping beats more elaborate constructions**.

- **A fixed-amplitude, localized school repulsion is especially effective for minimizing F2.**  
  The current best reaches **F2=0.49**, substantially better than the prior competitive methods: **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path** (**0.74**), **post_smoothing_base_pull** (**0.76**), **Gaussian Bias Polyline Path** (**0.9938**), and **heuristic_gaussian_path** (**1.34**). The implementation detail that stands out is that school avoidance is not proportional to school distance in y; instead it uses a **constant 35.0 vertical displacement with only Gaussian x-localization**, which appears to produce a much stronger school-noise reduction.

- **Sequential asymmetric treatment of school and base remains a high-value pattern.**  
  The current best first pushes away from the school, then applies base attraction with `y += (y_b - y) * w_b`. This means the base term acts on the **already school-adjusted path**, unlike more symmetric formulations such as **Gaussian School-Avoid Base-Seeking Path** (score **55.28**), which separately adds proportional school and base terms to the baseline. The sequential asymmetry appears to preserve the strong school-avoidance benefit while still partially recovering signal-loss behavior.

- **Low-complexity heuristics continue to outperform shaped arches, smoothing-heavy variants, and hand-tuned multi-term blends.**  
  Even among the newly listed programs, simpler Gaussian rules perform best: **22.67** for **Gaussian School Avoidance with Base Attraction** versus **30.34** for **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path**, **40.47** for **Gaussian Bias Polyline Path**, **41.88** for **Sinusoidal Baseline Drone Path**, and **55.28** for **Gaussian School-Avoid Base-Seeking Path**. This extends the earlier pattern that **direct geometric perturbation is the most robust successful template**.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Hardcoded global arches that ignore the actual school/base geometry remain weak, especially on F3.**  
  **Sinusoidal Baseline Drone Path** scored **41.88** with **F3=44.82**, much worse than the current best’s **22.67** and **F3=33.99**. Its fixed `-37.1 * sin(pi * pct)` offset did not use `school` or `base` meaningfully, and evaluator feedback explicitly noted that **signal loss dominated**. This reinforces the prior evidence that decorative sinusoidal shaping is inferior to geometry-aware Gaussian deformation.

- **Making both school and base effects proportional and symmetric tends to weaken the strongest objective gains.**  
  **Gaussian School-Avoid Base-Seeking Path** scored **55.28** with **F2=1.34**, far worse than the current best’s **0.49**. Its school term scaled with `(sy - y_line)` rather than using a fixed signed offset, which appears to have reduced the decisive localized avoidance effect. Relative to the current best, this suggests that **school repulsion works better as a fixed-amplitude local displacement than as a balanced proportional field**.

- **Extra conflict handling, local width tuning, and deviation clamps can over-constrain useful deformation.**  
  **Gaussian Bias Polyline Path** added local widths, capped amplitudes, conflict adjustment, and final deviation clamping, yet only reached **40.47** with **F3=34.14** and **F2=0.99**. Compared with the current best’s much simpler formulation, these safeguards seem to preserve smoothness at the cost of the stronger school/base geometry that the evaluator rewards.

- **Post-smoothing and conservative base pull improve regularity but do not necessarily beat raw Gaussian shaping.**  
  **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path** with two rounds of neighbor smoothing and a weaker post-smoothing base pull scored **30.34**. Although already competitive, it still loses clearly to the current best’s **22.67**. This aligns with the earlier global insight that **smoothing can dilute beneficial deformation**, especially when the evaluator seems tolerant of modestly higher path-length cost in exchange for stronger objective gains elsewhere.

## Implementation Insights
- **What makes the current best especially effective is the exact two-stage update order.**  
  It computes `y_line`, then applies school repulsion, then computes base attraction from the updated `y`:  
  `y += push_dir * 35.0 * w_s`  
  `y += (y_b - y) * w_b`  
  This is not equivalent to simply summing two baseline-centered offsets. The base term becomes a **partial correction after avoidance**, which likely helps retain the large school-noise improvement while avoiding overcommitment toward the base.

- **The current best uses a very simple but high-impact asymmetry: fixed school displacement, proportional base pull.**  
  This mirrors and strengthens prior observations from the earlier best Gaussian family. The school term is **constant-amplitude and sign-based**, while the base term is **distance-proportional toward `y_b`**. Compared with **heuristic_gaussian_path** and **Gaussian School-Avoid Base-Seeking Path**, where both terms were more similarly formulated, the current best obtains dramatically better overall score and much lower F2.

- **The kernel choice remains x-localized Gaussian weighting with no iterative search, no curvature term, and no explicit optimizer.**  
  Like the previous best families, the current program samples uniformly in x and independently computes each interior y. The actual code is extremely short, with both influence widths effectively set by the same denominator `400.0`. Despite this simplicity, it surpasses more engineered variants such as **Gaussian Bias Polyline Path** and the earlier surrogate-search-based methods mentioned in the prior insights.

- **The current best shows that stronger raw avoidance can be worthwhile even when F1 increases.**  
  Its **F1=1.36** is worse than **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path** (**1.28** in prior best summary, or **1.17** in the Generation 14 listing) and worse than **Gaussian Bias Polyline Path** (**1.1930**), but this higher distance cost is offset by a very large gain in **F2**. This indicates that the evaluator rewards the specific shape induced by the strong school Gaussian enough to dominate the modest path-length penalty.

## Performance Analysis
- **The current best establishes a new benchmark by a large margin.**  
  **Gaussian School Avoidance with Base Attraction** scores **22.67**, beating the previously highlighted best **Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path** at **29.85** by about **7.18 points**. That is a substantial improvement in this score range and is driven primarily by **F2 dropping from 0.74 to 0.49**, while **F3** remains in the low-to-mid 30s.

- **Among the evaluated correct programs, the ranking strongly favors simple Gaussian school/base deformation, with the current best clearly at the top.**  
  From the programs listed here, the ordering is: **22.67** (Gaussian School Avoidance with Base Attraction) < **30.34** (Gaussian Potential Field Drone Path) < **40.47** (Gaussian Bias Polyline Path) < **41.88** (Sinusoidal Baseline Drone Path) < **55.28** (Gaussian School-Avoid Base-Seeking Path). This continues the long-running pattern from earlier evaluations, but the current best widens the gap.

- **The new best does not win by minimizing F3; it wins by pushing F2 unusually low while keeping F3 merely competitive.**  
  Its **F3=33.99** is actually slightly worse than the earlier global best’s **31.35**, and similar to **Generation 14** (**34.11**) and **Generation 13** (**34.14**). But its **F2=0.49** is dramatically better than every compared method. So the score improvement appears to come from a **better overall tradeoff**, not from solving the signal-loss term outright.

- **Across the whole history, the biggest consistent separator remains whether the method is a direct geometry-aware Gaussian heuristic or something more stylized or overprocessed.**  
  The current best and prior best both fit the same successful template: straight baseline + localized Gaussian school avoidance + broad Gaussian base attraction. In contrast, sinusoidal arches, smoothing-heavy approaches, multi-stage modifiers, and symmetric potential blends all score worse. The new result strengthens the prior global conclusion that the evaluator most strongly rewards **simple, asymmetric Gaussian path shaping aligned to school/base geometry**.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Keep the exact sequential update order, but tune the two Gaussian widths independently instead of sharing `400.0`.**  
   The current best likely benefits from a narrow, high-impact school zone and a somewhat broader base recovery zone, so mutate to `w_s = exp(-dx_s^2 / sigma_s2)` and `w_b = exp(-dx_b^2 / sigma_b2)` with `sigma_s2 < sigma_b2`. This directly preserves the winning “repel first, then attract” structure while giving the base term more room to reduce F3 without weakening the sharp F2 gain.

2. **Replace the fixed `35.0` school push with a geometry-aware amplitude that stays large only when baseline school conflict is high.**  
   For example, scale the repulsion by how close `y_line` is to `y_s`, such as `amp_s = A0 + A1 * exp(-((y_line - y_s)^2)/tau)` or a clipped inverse-distance rule. This keeps the successful fixed-sign localized avoidance pattern, but may cut unnecessary detour cost in easier cases and improve the F1/F3 tradeoff.

3. **Make the school push direction base-aware when the school and base are on opposite sides of the baseline.**  
   Instead of always using only `y_s > y_line`, test a rule that biases the push toward the side that also moves the path closer to `y_b`, e.g. combine school-avoidance sign with `sign(y_b - y_line)` when conflict is ambiguous. This is a natural extension of the best program’s asymmetry and could reduce how much the later base attraction has to “undo” the school displacement.

4. **Add a very small second-stage base recovery Gaussian after the main update, centered tightly near `x_b`.**  
   Concretely, after `y += (y_b - y) * w_b`, try `y += alpha * (y_b - y) * exp(-((x - x_b)^2)/sigma_b2_post)` with small `alpha` and narrower support than the main base kernel. Prior results suggest generic smoothing hurts, but a targeted local recovery near the base may improve F3 while preserving the strong raw deformation that drives low F2.

5. **Condition base-attraction strength on how much school repulsion was actually applied at that x.**  
   Use the already-computed `w_s` to modulate the base term, such as weakening base pull slightly where school influence is strongest and strengthening it again outside that region. This keeps the successful sequential formulation intact, but may better preserve the school-avoidance dip near `x_s` while improving signal-loss recovery elsewhere.
